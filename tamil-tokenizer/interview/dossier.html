1. The 90-second answer

I built a byte-level BPE tokenizer for Tamil in Rust, and then spent most of the effort proving that it actually helps.

The problem: general-purpose tokenizers have almost no Tamil subwords, so Tamil text collapses toward one token per UTF-8 byte. Tamil is a three-byte script, so a Tamil word costs 10 to 14 tokens where an English word costs about 1.3. That is the tokenizer tax: slower inference, higher API cost, faster context exhaustion.

My approach had two halves. First, a Tamil-aware base vocabulary of 1,135 tokens built from the script itself: the 256 byte tokens, the 12 independent vowels, the 23 consonants, the 276 consonant-plus-vowel-sign clusters, the 528 conjunct stems, and the ligatures. Conjunct stems are the part that mattered. Tamil joins consonants constantly, so க்ஷ and ண்ட used to cost three or four tokens each. I also measured the obvious next step, 6,348 more tokens with vowel signs attached, and rejected it: the base vocabulary grew to about 7,000 and it ate the merge budget, making held-out compression worse.

Second, systems work in Rust. The trainer counts pairs in packed 64-bit keys and runs on rayon. The encoder replaced a hash map with a compressed sparse row layout after profiling showed the map stopped fitting in cache at 120,000 merges. It runs at 47 MB/s on a batch.

Then the part I would defend hardest. The Rust encoder and the Hugging Face export disagreed on 20 percent of lines for six commits. The cause was architectural: each side built its own model. I fixed it by putting both on one shared canonical model, which rebuilds the 819 base clusters out of single characters using promotion merges. Both engines now emit identical IDs on 100 percent of inputs, and the publish script refuses to upload an artifact that disagrees.

And then I measured whether any of it mattered. Bits per byte, not loss per token, because loss per token cannot compare tokenizers with different vocabulary sizes. On a matched 20 million token budget our model scores 0.9193 bits per byte on Tamil Wikipedia against 1.0123 for indictok-256k and 1.2575 for mBERT. On six-class Tamil news headline classification, 55.8 percent against 39.3 and 39.4. I also ran the control that a critic would ask for: the same 250 MB of text for every tokenizer. The ranking did not move.

The transcript corpus, in your own words

I did not scrape YouTube in this repo. scripts/fetch_transcripts.py downloads a pre-built JSONL that a sibling repo produced with youtube-transcript-api. I verified its provenance by reading the two scripts that produced it: one iterates 2,755 video IDs and groups captions by generated language, the other pulls the Tamil track and sleeps ten seconds between videos because the API bans the IP after a few hundred requests. From 1,185,664 raw captions, a Tamil gate that requires at least 30 characters, at least 40 percent Tamil letters and at most 2 percent markup characters keeps 896,641.

The split is by video, not by line, and that is the important part. Captions from one video are contiguous utterances of one speaker, so a line-level split would put near-duplicate sentences on both sides and make the held-out score meaningless. I hash the video ID and bucket it, which gives 278 test videos and 1,122 train videos, so no speaker appears in both halves.

What it bought: the transcripts column went up 11.9 percent, and on the Latin-heavy subset, where English sits inside Tamil words like thatடiled, the model went from losing to indictok-256k by 2 percent to leading it by 11 percent. That is the reason the corpus was worth fetching, and it is a case where adding training data beat adding vocabulary size.

Three corpus findings that changed a decision
  1. Domain skew is real and asymmetric. At 120K the wiki-only model loses 9.1 percent against the mix on IndicCorp, while the IndicCorp-only model loses only 1.4 percent on wiki. Wiki is the better general-purpose Tamil corpus because it is homogeneous, so its frequent n-grams are core orthography that transfers.
  2. Volume is irrelevant. Diversity is what costs. Retraining wiki on the same 25 MB repeated 8 times produces a byte-identical model. The trainer keys on unique words, so upsampling changes nothing. Only added diversity changes the model.
  3. Mixing only pays above about 100K merges. At 30K the mix loses to wiki-only on wiki. At 120K it ties the best specialist on each domain, inside 0.6 percent. That is a tie, not a win, and the earlier revision of the table overstated it as a win on both.
The held-out contamination you should know about

The transcripts split has a leak: 9,000 lines, 5.05 percent of the test half, appear in both halves. The cause is order-dependent deduplication, where a line seen first in the test split is never removed when the same text arrives later in a train video. The effect on the headline is small, moving the published model from 14.050 to 14.032 bytes per token, but it is real and the script's own comment claims to prevent it. If you are asked about data hygiene, this is the honest answer, and you can say the fix is to dedup against the full train set after the split rather than within it.

6. Question bank with follow-ups

Q1. Walk me through this project.

Answer: See section 1. Lead with the tokenizer tax, then the base vocabulary, then parity, then the measurement.

Follow-up 1: Why is the base vocabulary 1,135 tokens and not smaller? Because BPE training has a fixed merge budget, and every base token is a merge that cannot be spent learning corpus words. The measurement is in the rejection of the 6,348 extra clusters: the base reached about 7,000 and held-out compression went from 10.156 to 10.855.

Follow-up 2: How do you know the base vocabulary is not just overfitting? It was measured on three domains including one, Grantha-heavy text, that the base was not built for, and it improved there too. And the whole point of the base is that it works with zero training, which is a separate claim from it being a good training seed.

Q2. Why Rust instead of Python?

Answer: Two reasons. Memory layout and throughput predictability. BPE training and encoding do millions of pair lookups and priority queue operations. In Python every string and tuple is a heap object with reference counting, and the garbage collector introduces pauses. In Rust I pack pair keys into native 64-bit integers so they sit in CPU registers with no allocation, I use rayon to scale corpus ingestion and batch encoding across all cores with no data races, and I replaced the standard hash map merge table with a CSR index that avoids cache misses.

Follow-up 1: What is the actual speedup over a Python implementation? I do not have a like-for-like Python BPE trainer measured against this one, so I will not claim a ratio. What I can quote is this: the Rust trainer does 120,000 merges over a 293 MB corpus in 6.56 seconds, and the encoder sustains 47 MB/s batched.

Follow-up 2: What did Rust cost you? The borrow checker, mostly. The compile-time error messages about lifetimes in the promotion merge recursion in model.rs were the slowest part of the project. The payoff is that the encoder has no locks and no allocator traffic in the hot loop, so the throughput numbers are reproducible.

Q3. What makes Tamil tokenization hard?

Answer: Tamil is agglutinative and written in an abugida. Consonants carry an inherent short 'a' unless a vowel sign changes it, and the pulli suppresses that vowel to join the next consonant. Tamil also glues suffixes onto words. So if a tokenizer starts strictly at the Unicode character level, every cluster has to be assembled by learned merges, and words fragment into sub-syllabic pieces that carry no meaning for the model.

Follow-up 1: Give a concrete example. க்ஷ and ஸ்ரீ and ண்ட. Each is a consonant plus pulli plus consonant. Before I added the conjunct stems to the base vocab, each of those cost 3 to 4 tokens.

Follow-up 2: How do you know this matters for model quality, not just token count? Two ways. First, morphological alignment: MorphScore over 1,195 Tamil word forms from the UD_Tamil-TTB treebank gives us 0.709 recall against 0.480 for mBERT, so our boundaries land on morpheme edges more often. Second, the downstream classification result, where we score 16.5 points above both competitors and they collapse to zero F1 on the two minority classes. The honest caveat is that the literature does not fully support morphological alignment as the causal explanation, and I will say that too.

Q4. What was the hardest bug you hit?

Answer: The merge heap eviction bug in the trainer. When a pair merges, the frequencies of adjacent pairs change, so the binary heap holds entries with stale counts. The original code popped a stale entry and discarded it. That is catastrophic specifically because a max-heap pops the highest count first, so the entries that go stale at the top are exactly the frequent pairs in the corpus. The trainer permanently ignored its top candidates and spent merges on second-best ones. I fixed it with lazy validation: when a popped entry's count does not match the frequency table, re-push it with the corrected count. The fix was worth 25 percent of the token count, taking the model from 10.156 to 13.468 bytes per token at 30K merges, with 44,642 corrections over 30,000 merges.

Follow-up 1: Why did it take so long to notice? Because the bug made training faster. It skipped work. And the artifact it produced was a valid-looking model, just a worse one. The first sign was that a 30K merge run finished in 1.4 seconds on a 38 MB corpus, which was too fast.

Follow-up 2: How do you stop it coming back? There is a brute-force greedy BPE oracle in the test suite that recomputes the merges independently and compares. And there was a CI job that re-introduced each of the two trainer defects and required the suite to fail, because a regression test that no longer catches its own bug is worse than no test. That CI job was later dropped in a docs cleanup, which is a gap I know about.

Q5. How did you get Rust and Python to agree?

Answer: The two engines disagreed on 20 percent of lines for six commits, and I would have defended the export if asked to. The cause was architectural rather than a bug: each side built its own model. The export added byte tokens and promotion merges the encoder never saw, and the encoder seeded a chunk with the greedy longest base cluster while Hugging Face BPE always seeds with single characters. Two models produce two tokenizations.

Three things I tried and rejected. Registering the base clusters as Hugging Face added_tokens measured 0 percent agreement, because HF extracts added tokens before the BPE model runs, so the model never fires and output collapses to characters. Keeping HF's ByteLevel pretokenizer measured 0 percent agreement too, because it byte-maps the text before BPE sees it, so none of our raw-text merges can ever match. Changing only the export got me from 0 to 71.8 percent on wiki and 73.0 percent on IndicCorp, and it stalled there.

What worked was admitting the encoder was the side that was wrong. Hugging Face BPE has no seed-symbol concept, so the encoder cannot keep greedy longest-match seeding if it wants to be equivalent. I deleted the Aho-Corasick base automaton, made the encoder seed one symbol per character, made it merge every occurrence of the best-ranked pair in one pass, and put both sides on one shared canonical_model() that builds the vocabulary, the byte tokens and the promotion merges. Promotion merges rebuild each base cluster from characters and rank ahead of the learned merges, 819 of them. Both engines now emit identical IDs on 100 percent of inputs, and the token count difference is 0.0 percent, not 1.7 percent.

Follow-up 1: What is a promotion merge? A synthetic merge rule that rebuilds a base cluster out of single characters, so that HF's character-seeded BPE can reach it. For ஸ்ரீ the rules are ர plus ீ to make ரீ, then ஸ plus ரீ to make ஸ்ரீ. They rank ahead of the learned merges so the cluster exists before any learned merge that consumes it.

Follow-up 2: Why not just tell people to use the Rust encoder? That was the fallback position for six commits and it is a bad answer for a published artifact. The fix is better. The publish script now refuses to upload unless ID agreement is at least 99 percent and decode fidelity is 100 percent.

Follow-up 3: What was the subtle bug in the export? The decoder. It was a sequence of ByteFallback then ByteLevel. ByteFallback assembles <0xNN> into bytes and returns a UTF-8 string, and then ByteLevel re-applies the GPT-2 table to that output. A decoded é, which is itself a GPT-2 stand-in for byte 0xE9, got turned back into the raw byte and then decoded to U+FFFD. That is silent corruption rather than a token count problem, and it took a separate fix: ByteFallback alone, never ByteLevel after it.

Q6. How do you know the compression advantage is real?

Answer: Compression alone proves nothing about model quality, so I ran three things. Bits per byte instead of loss per token, because a tokenizer that packs more text into a token has fewer tokens to spread the loss over, so per-token loss is not comparable across vocabularies. Three seeds per tokenizer, 1234, 1235 and 1236, with the spread reported alongside the mean. And the byte-matched control, which is the one a critic would ask for: the same 250 MB of the same corpus for every tokenizer, because a fixed token budget hands the better compressor more text and cannot separate segmentation from coverage.

The control is where the argument is made. Under a fixed token budget we score 0.9193 bits per byte on wiki against 1.0123 for indictok-256k and 1.2575 for mBERT, and we saw 309 MB of text against 257 MB and 129 MB. Under the byte-matched control we score 0.9190, they score 1.0123 and 1.2573. Nothing moved. The informative row is mBERT: matching bytes nearly doubled its training text from 129 MB to 250 MB and its bits per byte did not move at all, 1.2575 to 1.2573. More coverage was not what it lacked.

Follow-up 1: Is your comparison fair against indictok-256k? Partly. Its 256,000-token vocabulary makes its embedding table 109.1M parameters against our 58.0M, so some of what it loses is vocabulary size rather than tokenization. I would rather state that than hide it. The clean comparison is against mBERT, where the sizes are nearly identical: 119,547 tokens against 122,723, and 56.8M parameters against 58.0M. We win with half the parameters of indictok, which is the opposite of what the size difference predicts.

Follow-up 2: What are the caveats? The byte-matched control is one seed per row, though its margins sit within 0.1 points of the three-seed range. A 58M-parameter model on 20M tokens is heavily undertrained, which is why the absolute bits-per-byte numbers are high. And the classification runs vary the fine-tune seed, not the pretraining seed.

Q7. What trade-offs did you uncover?

Answer: Two, and both are in the docs because they are measured costs rather than hidden ones.

The first is compression against morphological alignment. The dummy prefix, a space prepended to every training line, was worth 1.29 percent compression because it stops line-initial words from splitting differently from words in the middle of a line. It also drops MorphScore recall from 0.6352 to 0.5129, so minus 19 percent. That was the right call on the evidence available at the time, because compression was measurable and alignment was not. Now both are measured, so it is a price rather than a surprise.

The second is vocabulary size against merge budget. 120K merges buys 13 to 26 percent compression for about 7 to 10 percent throughput, but it also means 21.3 percent of the vocabulary is never used on any held-out split and the last 30K merges carry 2.3 percent of emitted tokens. That is not an argument for shrinking, since a 120K vocabulary partly exists to cover rare-but-correct input, but it does mean the advantage is concentrated in the first 60 to 90K merges. The smallest vocabulary that covers 99.9 percent of emitted tokens is 93,835.

Follow-up 1: Would you ship differently now? The 120K model still beats every smaller variant on both compression and alignment, so I would ship it. If alignment mattered more than compression for a specific downstream use, I would ship the 30K wiki model instead, because its alignment is better and its throughput is better.

Follow-up 2: What else did you price but not decide? Splitting digits into individual chunks. I measured it end to end: it costs 3.4 percent compression on wiki and 1.5 percent on IndicCorp. The benefit, that a model has to compose digits rather than memorise whole numbers, is a downstream claim and this repo has no arithmetic benchmark, so I priced the option rather than resolving it. Anyone with that harness should re-run it. It is a one-line pretokenizer change.

Q8. How does the pretokenizer work?

Answer: It is one regex with alternatives, each introduced by a pipe. Special tokens first, then words with apostrophes, then underscore words, then uppercase sequences, then lowercase or mixed, then Tamil and Indic letters, then numbers, then punctuation and symbols, then whitespace, then separators and controls, then stray combining marks. The alternatives are ordered so the more specific ones win.

The character classes use Unicode properties. \p{L} is any letter, \p{M} is any combining mark, and Tamil is a letter plus combining marks, which is why the Tamil pattern is (?:[\p{Lt}\p{Lm}\p{Lo}]\p{M}*)+.

Follow-up 1: Why does pretokenization matter at all? Because it sets the walls that merges cannot cross. Without it, BPE will happily merge a space into a word and then a word into the next one, and the segmentation stops corresponding to anything in the language. It also caps chunk length, which is why the rescan-based merge loop is fast enough.

Follow-up 2: What was the bug? A missing alternation. Two rules concatenated without a pipe became one rule instead of two alternatives, so the pretokenizer merged them into a single pattern that matched the wrong thing. It was one character.

Follow-up 3: Why not use HF's ByteLevel pretokenizer? Because it hardcodes GPT-2's pattern and byte-maps text before BPE sees it, so none of our raw-text merges could ever match. That was worth 0 percent ID agreement when I tried it. The export uses a Split with our own regex instead.

Q9. Why BPE and not Unigram?

Answer: I measured it rather than assuming. I built a SentencePiece reference harness that trains Unigram and BPE at the same vocabulary size on the same corpus with the same seed set and the same normalization, because a comparison where the candidate sets differ measures the seed rather than the objective. Unigram beat BPE by 0.64 percent on wiki and 0.67 percent on IndicCorp. That is a tie.

I also tried writing a Unigram trainer, which was worse by a factor of 1.8 because I used hard Viterbi counts instead of SentencePiece's forward-backward marginals and pruned by raw frequency instead of the loss. 26.6 percent of its emitted tokens were single characters. It was deleted before it was ever committed.

The decision was: 0.6 percent does not pay for a second trainer and a second export path, especially when BPE's deterministic rank-based merges are what Hugging Face's standard inference path implements. I also want to be honest that the usual arguments for Unigram are morphological alignment and downstream model quality, and I did not test either of those in that harness.

Follow-up 1: Where are those numbers? They were in IMPROVEMENT_ROADMAP.md, which I deleted in a docs cleanup at b14d245. They are recoverable with git show b14d245^:IMPROVEMENT_ROADMAP.md. That deletion was a mistake and I would not repeat it.

Q10. How do you handle characters the model has never seen?

Answer: Byte fallback, on both sides, and it is the reason the tokenizer is lossless. Every one of the 256 byte values has a token, spelled <0xNN> the way Hugging Face spells them, and any character not in the vocabulary is emitted as one token per UTF-8 byte. So encoding never drops anything and decoding is byte-exact, for any input including emoji, Han characters and malformed UTF-8.

Follow-up 1: How does that interact with the GPT-2 byte table? The base vocabulary's byte tokens use GPT-2's byte-to-unicode table exactly, because Hugging Face's ByteLevel pretokenizer and decoder hardcode it. The export uses the <0xNN> spelling instead, with a ByteFallback decoder, and never ByteLevel after it, because that combination double-maps Latin-1 and produces replacement characters.

Follow-up 2: Is there a case where it hurts? Yes, one that I found and did not fix: on text where Latin-1 characters are literal text, our engine emits their UTF-8 bytes while HF emits the stand-in itself. The token counts match but the IDs differ by design. The verification script triages these separately from real mismatches.

Q11. What would you do differently?

Answer: Four things, in order of how much they bother me.

  1. I deleted the decision log too early. IMPROVEMENT_ROADMAP.md held the BPE versus Unigram table, the digit-splitting measurement and the pre- and post-fix compression grid. When I split the README into docs/ I deleted it, and several of those measurements now exist only in git history. A docs restructure should not delete measurement records.
  2. I left stale numbers in the code. The export_hf.rs header still says ID parity is 79.2 percent. It is 100 percent. The paragraph explains why the gap should close, and the gap closed in the commit that rewrote the encoder. A comment that says "this is broken because of the old design" outlives the old design unless someone checks it.
  3. I dropped a CI guard. The job that re-introduced each trainer defect and required the test suite to fail was removed in a docs cleanup, because it lived in the same file. That guard is why the heap bug and the ID-minting bug would not come back.
  4. The transcripts split has a 5 percent leak from order-dependent deduplication, 9,000 lines. The fix is to dedup against the full train set after the split, not within each half.
Q12. Why should I trust these numbers?

Answer: Because the harness re-derives rather than storing. scripts/publish_hf.py refuses to upload unless decode fidelity is 100 percent and ID agreement is at least 99 percent. The measurement scripts re-run the comparison rather than reading a stored value. The downstream numbers are means over three seeds with the range printed next to them, and the within-tokenizer spread is at most 0.0022 bits per byte against a smallest gap of 0.072, so the ranking is 33 times beyond the seed noise. The classification result has a paired bootstrap over 10,000 resamples and both confidence intervals exclude zero.

And I can reproduce the published artifact. Rebuilding the mix from the three train splits and retraining gives byte-identical merges and vocabulary, and the exported tokenizer.json has the same SHA-256 as the file on the Hub.

9. What you genuinely cannot answer

Knowing what you do not have is worth as much as knowing what you do. Each of these is a real limit, and saying so out loud is a strength, not a gap.

  • Does the segmentation cause the downstream gain, or is it the data? Unanswered. The byte-matched control rules out coverage as the explanation, but MUTANT-Indic (2026) found that 2.4x better Tamil fertility produced an essentially identical downstream score. My data is consistent with segmentation mattering and does not prove it.
  • Does morphological alignment explain the performance gap? No, and the literature is fairly clear. Arnett and Bergen (COLING 2025) find some evidence that tokenization quality explains the agglutinative-versus-fusional gap and none for morphological alignment, attributing it to dataset size instead. Arnett, Hudspeth and O'Connor (ICML 2025) reach the same conclusion across 70 languages and 7 tasks. Alignment describes the boundaries, it is not evidence they are the reason a model performs better.
  • Does splitting digits help arithmetic? Unpriced on the benefit side. The compression cost is measured at 1.5 to 3.4 percent, the arithmetic benefit is not, because the harness does not measure arithmetic. It is a one-line change and a retrain for anyone who cares.
  • How much does pretraining seed variance matter? Only the fine-tune seeds were varied in the classification runs. The token-budget bits-per-byte table has three pretraining seeds. The classification table varies fine-tuning, not pretraining, and the fine-tune spread is large, up to 0.13 accuracy, which is why the ranges and the paired bootstrap are there.
  • Is the 79.2 percent figure wrong? No, it was right once, for six commits. It is wrong now. The honest version of the story is that I fixed the encoder, not the export, and the stale comment is still in the file.
